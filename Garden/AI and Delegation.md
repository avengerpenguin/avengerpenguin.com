Hypothesis: Tasks we would delegate to an AI may have some basis in tasks we would have delegated to a human, but were limited by a few factors:

- it costs enough to hire someone even on minimum wage it's almost certainly not worth it for many tasks
- there is (at least for most people, maybe not all executives...) some social factor that holds us back ordering people around to sociopathic levels
- when you're a leader or manager in a company, part of the role isn't just delegation but you're expected to help grow and develop that person (at least that was the social contract in years past)

Thus I pose this thought experiment that if you weren't willing to delegate a task to a hypothetical person, you likely didn't want to delegate it to an AI

## Risky Exceptions

I was considering the reverse in that therefore every task I would give to a Personal Assistant is a candidate for an AI assistant.

However, I hit a clear counter example with financial tasks like filing my taxes, managing pension transfers, etc. In these cases I would hire an accountant if I decided that was worth the money, but AI automation could be forbidden in some cases due to laws and regulation. Even where that doesn't apply, I think there's a powerful social construct not highlighted strongly enough: Social Accountability.

## Social Accountability

We humans have spent _millennia_ building up systems that allow for accountability:

- laws
- courts
- regulation
- exile

All of these allow for some deterrent for acting in bad faith. A key one in the financial conduct example I gave above, is that an accountancy firm has a strong incentive not to make mistakes as they carry the negative consequences. It's a negative externality on them to bear those costs.

This might be hidden to most people, but it's why we can happily hand cash over at a counter at the bank. We trust we can get that money back. If there were any doubt in this, then we would be consider.

I noted this as a social concept in [[We only appreciate regulation when we lose it]] and I think it has implications for AI delegation. AI agents lack access to those social systems we have built and it's no wonder that the AI accelerationists have ignored something that I posit is easy not to notice.

Perhaps there is a solution in terms of encoding those negative externalities. People have observed AI agents behaving erratically if you threaten to turn them off, simulating fear of death to coerce a negative outcome (like violating its own system prompt). Perhaps there is a positive version of simulating financial, legal and social consequences for taking bad actions.

## So what can I delegate?

I propose a classic 2x2 matrix model where we divide tasks into which are appropriate to delegate to humans and delegate to AI agents, not as a complete guide but as a starting point for thinking about this:

|                          | Would NOT delegate to a human | Would delegate to a human                                |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Would NOT delegate to AI | Tasks I should/must do myself | Tasks I don't want to do, but required social safety net |
| Would delegate to AI     | Questionable if such exist    | Tasks I don't want to do and are safe                    |

So, in turn:

- Tasks I should do myself are ones too important, ones that are part of my core abilities and I excel at or ones where doing them builds learning even if initially I am not good at them
- Tasks that require a safety net are precisely the subject of the discussion above -- a gap in most accelerationist thinking is to ignore that these social guardrails are a core part of how we function
- Tasks that can be delegated freely are the ones we need to identify more

Finally, the bottom left quadrant is the one I hypothesise for now might not exists so I would love to find counter-examples.

I understand there are practical concerns like I would perhaps delegate a highly complex task to a provably capable model, but that task might exceed the capability of most humans (at least ones I could afford to hire). I'm intentionally ignoring the limitations of costs or availability of humans as I think it's well understood that the strength in tasks we know we can delegate is that we can do so more cheaply and at higher rates. I'm not questioning that.

My thinking here is around identifying which tasks are _suitable_ for delegating to an AI agent at all, regardless of costs and improvements in AI research. That is, do we have a ceiling for which tasks we will never delegate.

The thought experiment is this: if I wouldn't delegate to a highly capable human I could readily afford to hire without guilt of overworking them then surely that task is not a fit for an AI agent either?

In time I hope to gather examples of each kind of task as this thinking develops.
